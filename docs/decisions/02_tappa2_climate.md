# Tappa 2 — Monthly climate (temperature and precipitation)

Status: **closed**. Twelve monthly temperature and precipitation layers at
120 m over the full domain, generated by `run_tappa2_climate.py` from the
v3 DEM. Precipitation uses Smith & Barstad (2004) linear orographic theory;
temperature is a closed-form latitude / lapse / continentality model.
Both are validated against the Tappa 0 reference region (South Island, NZ).

This document is the decision record for the stage, per the project's
per-stage workflow. As with `01_tappa1_terrain.md` it is written to work
either as a log of what was decided, or as a reusable recipe.

## 1. Wind direction — the one decision that had to come first

### 1a. A correction to the Tappa 0 reasoning

`00_pre_project_planning.md` states: *"if diagonal, the wind vector (Tappa 2)
must be set perpendicular to whatever exact angle is drawn."* That has the
causality backwards, and following it would have broken consistency with two
choices already locked in.

The domain is centred on **44° S**. That is inside the Southern Hemisphere
mid-latitude westerly belt — the Roaring Forties, the same belt the stated
validation reference (South Island, NZ) sits in. Latitude fixes the wind to
the western quadrant. The skeleton was already drawn in Tappa 1. So the wind
is not free to be rotated to suit the ridges; the only live question was
*where within the western quadrant*, and how much rain-shadow contrast each
choice buys.

### 1b. What the skeleton actually offers

The ridges are not a parallel range. Length-weighted axial concentration is
**R = 0.35** (1.0 = perfectly parallel) — a fan, not a chain — so no single
bearing is optimal for all of it. Orographic effectiveness, measured as the
elevation×length-weighted mean of `|sin(angle between wind and ridge axis)|`:

| wind from | overall | Spine (64 km, 3394 m) | weakest ridge |
|---|---|---|---|
| W 270° | 0.65 | 0.51 | West Branch 0.38 |
| **WSW 250°** | **0.77** | **0.77** | West Branch 0.67 |
| SW 230° | 0.81 | 0.94 | South Branch 0.60 |

Effectiveness peaks at 230–250°, **not** at due west — a pure westerly is
only 30° off the Spine's own 120° axis, so it crosses the dominant range
obliquely and would give a weak, illegible shadow across exactly the feature
the map is built around.

**Decision: 250° (WSW), locked.** Best compromise that stays defensible for
the latitude: no ridge below 0.67, and squarely inside the westerly belt.
230° buys a stronger Spine shadow but a south-westerly *annual mean* is hard
to justify at 44° S, and it costs the South Branch.

Consequence for later stages: the whole east/north-east side of the island is
the dry side. Tappa 5's biomes and Tappa 6's suitability inherit that.

## 2. Working resolution — 120 m, not 30 m

Twelve months × two variables at the DEM's native 30 m is ~2.2 GB, which is
not something to commit or to carry through four more stages. Climate fields
are intrinsically smooth: the precipitation model's own advection length,
`U·(τc+τf) ≈ 24 km`, is three orders of magnitude coarser than 30 m, so
there is no signal below ~1 km for it to lose. 120 m (a 4× block-mean of the
DEM, 1334 × 1084) is the working grid; the full stack is ~35 MB per variable
as int16.

Temperature is a closed-form function of the DEM, so it can be re-evaluated
at native 30 m for hero renders at any time without re-running anything —
`temperature_month()` takes whatever elevation grid it is handed.

Two things the coarsening has to get right (`src/climate/grid.py`):

* **Clip bathymetry before averaging, not after.** Air flows over the sea
  *surface*; averaging first would let a trench beside a headland cancel the
  headland's uplift. `coarsen()` returns `surface` (with bathymetry, for the
  temperature model) and `uplift_h` = block-mean of `max(dem, 0)` separately.
* The 1334 × 1084 grid overhangs the ROI's south and east edges by 80 m
  (the 30 m grid's own 20 m overhang × 4). Edge-replicated rather than
  trimmed, so the north-west origin stays exactly on `(xmin, ymax)` and the
  ENVI `map info` anchors cleanly.

## 3. Temperature model

    T(x,y,m) = T_sl(x,y,m) - gamma(m) * z(x,y)

    T_sl(x,y,m) = T_ref + dT_dlat*(lat(y) - lat_ref)
                  + A(x,y) * cos(2*pi*(m - m_peak(x,y))/12)

Building a *sea-level* field with its own seasonal cycle and only then
reducing it to the surface is the physically correct order, and it is what
allows the lapse rate itself to vary by month.

### 3a. Lapse rate — 5.0 °C/km, not 6.5

`config/parameters.yml` carried `lapse_rate_c_per_1000m: -6.5`. That is the
ICAO standard-atmosphere / global-average environmental lapse rate, and it
is the wrong value for this world's own stated validation reference.
Norton (1985) derived **5.0 °C/km** as the mean annual lapse rate from 301
New Zealand climate stations; it has been confirmed since at 5.0 (Franz
Josef, Anderson et al. 2006) and 5.4 with strong winter inversions (Ben
Ohau, Doughty 2013), and is the value used in the published Southern Alps
permafrost work. Maritime mid-latitude ranges are shallower than the global
average because the air crossing them is close to moist-adiabatic.

This is not a cosmetic change. At the 3596 m summit the two values differ by
**5.4 °C** — the difference between having a permanent snowline and not
having one, which Tappa 3 is built on.

Seasonal modulation is set to a deliberately conservative **±0.3 °C/km**
(steepest in February). The honest limitation: a single column lapse rate
cannot represent both winter *valley* inversions (a boundary-layer effect)
and free-atmosphere summit temperatures (about as seasonal as the surface).
Any positive amplitude trades the second for the first, damping the annual
range aloft by `2·amplitude·z`. At 0.7 the summit would end up with 3.0 °C of
annual range against the coast's 8.0 — too flat to defend. At 0.3 it keeps
~5.8 °C. Set to 0.0 for a strictly constant lapse rate; **Tappa 3's snow
metrics are where this choice actually bites**, so re-check it there.

### 3b. Continentality — real, and second-order, and measured

Max distance-to-coast on this island is **29.9 km** (mean 8.3 km, land area
9,913 km²). That is about a third of South Island NZ's width: this world is
strongly maritime by construction, and continentality can only ever be a
minor term here. It is modelled the standard way (as a function of distance
to sea, computed with `scipy.ndimage.distance_transform_edt` — GRASS
`r.grow.distance` from the Tappa 0 sketch is unavailable in this sandbox and
computes the identical quantity), acting on the annual **amplitude** and on
the **phase lag**, never on the annual mean. Continentality does not make a
place colder on average; it makes summers hotter, winters colder, and the
seasonal peak arrive earlier.

The e-folding scale is kept at a physically defensible 30 km rather than
shrunk to make the term look important. Measured result, at low elevation:
annual range **8.05 °C within 2 km of the shore → 10.74 °C at 16–30 km
inland**.

**A trap worth recording**: binned by distance-to-coast alone, the surface
annual range looks *flat* (7.98 → 8.22 °C). That is not the continentality
term failing. On this island "inland" and "high" are the same cells (mean
elevation goes 132 m → 2317 m across those bins), and the seasonal lapse
term damps the annual range with height by almost exactly as much as
continentality raises it. The two only separate when elevation is held
fixed. Both effects are real; their near-cancellation is a coincidence of
this particular geography.

### 3c. Locked-in parameters

```
t_ref_sea_level_c              = 11.5     # annual mean at sea level, 44 S
lat_ref_deg                    = -44.0
dt_dlat_c_per_deg              = 0.70     # ~1.0 C across the 1.437 deg span
lapse_rate_c_per_km            = 5.0
lapse_seasonal_amplitude_c_per_km = 0.3
lapse_peak_month               = 2.0      # steepest in late summer
amplitude_coastal_c            = 4.0
amplitude_inland_c             = 7.0
continentality_scale_km        = 30.0
peak_month_coastal             = 2.2      # maritime lag after the solstice
peak_month_shift_inland        = 0.9
```

An ellipsoidal inverse Lambert Conformal Conic (`grid.lcc_inverse_lat`,
Snyder 1987) supplies `lat(y)`. The latitude term is worth only ~1 °C over
the whole domain so a spherical approximation would have been defensible —
the ellipsoidal inverse is implemented anyway because getting the projection
maths right is part of what this case study is for. It costs about twenty
lines and runs once per grid. Sanity check: `y = 0` returns exactly −44.000°.

## 4. Precipitation — Smith & Barstad (2004)

Reference: Smith, R.B. & Barstad, I. (2004), "A linear theory of orographic
precipitation", *J. Atmos. Sci.* 61(12), 1377–1391.

### 4a. Why not the usual upslope heuristic

The standard worldbuilding recipe is `P ∝ dot(wind, grad(h))` plus an ad-hoc
leeward decay. It has three defects that matter for something meant to be
defended technically:

1. Precipitation lands exactly on the slope that produced the uplift. Real
   condensate is advected downwind while it forms and while it falls; the
   maximum is displaced, and by how much depends on wind speed.
2. The rain shadow has to be bolted on as a separate rule with its own free
   parameters, rather than emerging from the same physics.
3. There is no way to justify any particular constant.

Smith & Barstad solves the steady-state advection of vertically integrated
cloud water and hydrometeors over terrain, linearised about a uniform wind,
in Fourier space:

```
P_hat(k,l) = Cw · i·sigma · h_hat(k,l)
             / [ (1 - i·m·Hw)(1 + i·sigma·tau_c)(1 + i·sigma·tau_f) ]

sigma = u·k + v·l                                    intrinsic frequency
m     = sqrt( (Nm^2 - sigma^2)/sigma^2 · (k^2 + l^2) )  vertical wavenumber
```

Windward enhancement, the displacement of the maximum, and leeward drying
all fall out of that one expression, and every constant has a name, a unit,
and a measurable real-world range.

### 4b. The branch cut that decides whether it works at all

For vertically propagating waves (`sigma² < Nm²`) the vertical wavenumber
must carry energy *upward*, which fixes `sign(m) = sign(sigma)`. Getting
that branch wrong flips the field's upwind/downwind asymmetry — the rain
lands on the lee side and the shadow on the windward side, which looks
plausible enough at a glance to ship by accident. It is enforced explicitly
in `orographic_precip()` and verified in §4e.

### 4c. Coupling to the temperature model

`Cw` (uplift sensitivity) and `Hw` (moisture scale height) are **not** tuned
independently. Both are derived from each month's domain-mean sea-level
temperature via Clausius-Clapeyron and the moist adiabat:

```
Cw = rho_s(T) · Gamma_m(T) / gamma        [kg m-3]
Hw = Rv · T^2 / (L · gamma)               [m]
```

giving Cw = 0.0079 kg/m³ at 5 °C to 0.0121 at 15 °C, and Hw ≈ 2860–3070 m —
both inside S&B's published ranges (Cw 0.001–0.02, Hw ~2500). So the
seasonal precipitation cycle is a *consequence* of the seasonal temperature
and wind cycles rather than a third hand-tuned knob. Note both scale
inversely with the environmental lapse rate: the 5.0-vs-6.5 decision in §3a
raises Hw by ~30% and is not confined to the temperature model.

Sea-level temperature, not surface: Cw and Hw describe the incoming
airstream over the ocean upwind, not air already sitting on a 3 km summit.

### 4d. The one calibrated parameter, and why it has to exist

Run at face value the model returns **~170,000 mm/yr** on the windward flank.
That is not a bug: it is the instantaneous rate of a fully saturated 12 m/s
airstream lifted over the Spine — about **43 mm/hr**, a perfectly reasonable
heavy-rain rate and an absurd monthly mean. **Smith & Barstad is an event
model, not a climatology.** Real climatology is that rate times the fraction
of hours it is occurring.

So one parameter is introduced and calibrated: `orographic_duty_cycle = 0.07`,
the fraction of the month during which the modelled saturated upslope flow is
actually happening. Because the model is linear in `Cw`, applying it is
identical to using an effective `Cw = Cw_thermo · duty`, which is how it is
implemented. 7% of hours is plausible against NZ West Coast rain statistics,
and it is calibrated (see §5), not assumed.

Two further terms handle what linear theory does not model:

* `background_precip_mm_per_month = 60` — non-orographic (synoptic/frontal)
  rain, the ~720 mm/yr a flat island at this latitude gets anyway. Comparable
  to flat maritime NZ sites (Christchurch 612, Invercargill 1035). Additive
  and stated separately rather than folded into a fitted constant.
* `lee_floor_fraction = 0.45` — a cap on leeward suppression. Left to itself
  the linear solution drives the lee to −230,000 mm/yr equivalent, and the
  usual `max(P, 0)` truncation then sets **27% of the island's land area to
  exactly zero rainfall**, which no maritime mid-latitude place on Earth
  records. The unbounded drying is an artefact of the model having no
  moisture budget: it can remove more water than the airstream ever carried.
  Capping suppression at 45% of background puts the driest cells at 324 mm/yr
  against Alexandra's ~300. Applied as a softplus, not a hard `max`, so it
  does not stamp a flat plateau across the whole lee.

### 4e. Verification before calibration (`assets/02_model_verification.png`)

Run on an analytic 2 km / 10 km-half-width Gaussian ridge with a westerly,
each term switched on in turn. Expected behaviour, all confirmed:

| case | precipitation maximum | theory |
|---|---|---|
| Nm→0, τ=0 | −7.5 km | pure upslope: steepest windward slope, at −a/√2 = −7.07 km |
| + τ=1000 s | +2.5 km | fallout delay advects the rain downwind |
| + Nm=0.005 | −2.0 km | the mountain wave tilts the uplift upwind, partly cancelling |

The pure-upslope case is also exactly antisymmetric (max +51.3, min −51.3),
as it must be for an antisymmetric slope field. This is the check that the
branch cut in §4b is right.

### 4f. Numerical practicalities

* **FFT padding**: the domain is padded by 40 km and the terrain blended to
  zero with a raised cosine. Without it the transform's implicit periodicity
  lets the eastern lee wrap round and rain on the western windward coast —
  and land does reach all four domain edges on this DEM (320 cells on the
  north edge, 270 on the east). A *hard* zero edge would be no better: a step
  function injects broadband spectral energy, the same failure mode as the
  single-frequency domain warp in Tappa 1 §3d.
* `sigma = 0` (flow exactly parallel to a feature) is a removable singularity
  — no work done, no precipitation — set to zero explicitly.
* Whole run: 12 months of both variables in **~19 s** on two cores. Unlike
  Tappa 1 there is no reason to ship the outputs; regenerate them with
  `python run_tappa2_climate.py`.

### 4g. Locked-in parameters

```
wind_from_deg                     = 250.0
wind_speed_ms                     = 12.0
wind_speed_seasonal_amplitude_ms  = 2.0
wind_peak_month                   = 8.0    # SH jet equatorward in winter
nm_moist_stability                = 0.005  # s-1
tau_c_s = tau_f_s                 = 1000.0
orographic_duty_cycle             = 0.07   # the calibrated one
background_precip_mm_per_month    = 60.0
lee_floor_fraction                = 0.45
pad_km                            = 40.0
```

`wind_speed_seasonal_amplitude_ms` alone sets the seasonal precipitation
cycle, because the two seasonal drivers pull against each other: winter has
more wind but colder, drier air. Measured domain-mean winter:summer ratios —
0.76 at amplitude 0 (moisture wins outright), 1.02 at 1.5, **1.12 at 2.0**,
1.36 at 3.0. 2.0 lands near the reference region's behaviour (West Coast
rainfall close to uniform through the year, Canterbury a mild winter/autumn
maximum) without either a flat cycle or a monsoon-like one.

## 5. Validation against South Island, NZ (`assets/02_validation.png`)

| metric | this world | real South Island NZ |
|---|---|---|
| windward lowland (−40..−20 km along wind) | 2 402 mm | Greymouth 2 443 |
| leeward lowland (+20..+40 km) | 430 mm | Christchurch 612 |
| windward : leeward ratio | 5.6 : 1 | 4.0 : 1 |
| domain maximum | 12 320 mm | Southern Alps crest ~11–13 000 |
| domain minimum | 324 mm | Alexandra ~300 |
| land mean | 2 571 mm | South Island ~1 500–2 000 |
| annual mean T, coast | 11.3 °C | Hokitika / Christchurch 11.7–12.2 |
| annual T range, coast | 8.1 °C | Hokitika ~7.2 |
| annual T range, 16–30 km inland | 10.5 °C | Alexandra ~15 |
| 0 °C isotherm, coldest month | 1 410 m | Southern Alps winter ~1 200–1 600 |
| permanently sub-zero land (all 12 monthly means < 0) | 1.1% (108 km²) | Southern Alps perennial snow/ice |

Honest reading of that table: the totals and the vertical structure land
where they should. Two entries sit outside the reference, both for the same
structural reason — **this island is smaller and steeper than South Island**.
The windward:leeward ratio is high (5.6 vs 4.0) and the land mean is high
(2 571 vs ~1 750) because mean land elevation here is 919 m against South
Island's ~500 m, and because the lee "lowland" band is only ~20 km wide, so
it never gets far enough downwind for the shadow to recover. The inland
annual T range falls short (10.5 vs 15) for the mirror-image reason: nowhere
on this island is more than 30 km from the sea, and Alexandra is ~120 km
inland in a closed basin. Neither is a model error; both are geography, and
both are worth restating rather than tuning away.

The one number worth revisiting in Tappa 3 is the permanent-snow area
(1.1%, 108 km²). It is defined here as *all twelve monthly means below 0 °C*,
which sits far above the glaciological equilibrium line — real Southern Alps
glaciers descend well below their 0 °C-in-the-warmest-month surface because
accumulation there is enormous. Tappa 3's snowline metric should be
precipitation-aware, not a pure temperature threshold.

## 6. Outputs

`run_tappa2_climate.py` writes to `data/processed/climate/` (gitignored):

| file | contents |
|---|---|
| `temperature_monthly_c.npy` | (12, 1334, 1084) float32, °C |
| `precipitation_monthly_mm.npy` | (12, 1334, 1084) float32, mm/month |

| `precipitation_monthly_mm.bin/.hdr/.prj` | 12-band ENVI int16, whole mm |
| `annual_precipitation_mm.*` | single-band ENVI int16, whole mm |
| `annual_mean_temperature_c.*` | single-band ENVI **float32, plain °C** |
| `surface_elevation_m.npy`, `distance_to_coast_km.npy`, `land_mask.npy` | 120 m ancillaries for later stages |
| `climate_run_meta.json` | every parameter, every month's derived state, summary stats |

**Temperature is float32, deliberately.** The first cut of this stage
exported it as int16 in hundredths of a degree (`_c100`), reusing Tappa 1
§7's argument that int16 halves the file. That argument does not carry over.
Tappa 1 was halving a 92 MB DEM that took 25 minutes to generate and had to
be handed over as a single artefact; this stack is 69 MB instead of 35, it is
gitignored, and it regenerates in 19 s. The 35 MB was buying a raster whose
pixels read `-821` in the QGIS identify tool and silently meant −8.21 °C —
a unit convention the reader has to carry in their head to interpret any
value. Wrong trade; reverted. Precipitation stays int16 because whole
millimetres need no scaling to be readable.

If a `temperature_monthly_c100.*` or `annual_mean_temperature_c100.*` is
still sitting in `data/processed/climate/` from a run before this change,
delete it — the driver writes new names and will not clean up the old ones.

`src/terrain/raster_io.write_envi_raw` gained multi-band support for this,
so the twelve monthly layers ship as one raster QGIS opens with a band
selector rather than as twenty-four files. 2-D input behaves exactly as
before. Same CRS caveat as Tappa 1 §7: `map info` carries the affine
georeferencing, a `.prj` sidecar carries the PROJ4 string, and if QGIS still
loads with an unknown CRS, assign "Fictional World LCC" manually.

Summary of the run: annual precipitation 324 / 1 720 / 12 320 mm
(min / median / max on land), land mean 2 571 mm; annual mean temperature
−6.3 / 8.7 / 12.0 °C, land mean 6.9 °C; warmest-month land mean 11.3 °C,
coldest 2.6 °C.

## 7. Consequences for the site's visual plan

`00_pre_project_planning.md` proposes seasonal snapshots (2–4 representative
months) as a radio switcher for both variables. That works for temperature
and will work for the Tappa 3 snow layers — the 0 °C isotherm migrates from
~2 990 m in February to ~1 480 m in August, which is a genuinely different
map each time (`assets/02_seasonal.png`, top row).

**It does not work for precipitation.** With a fixed wind direction and a
12% seasonal amplitude, the precipitation *pattern* is essentially invariant
and only its magnitude moves; four monthly panels are four near-identical
pictures (`assets/02_seasonal.png`, bottom row). Show precipitation as one
annual map plus a small monthly bar chart, and spend the seasonal switcher
on temperature and snow.

The isolines-vs-choropleth question from Tappa 0 (item 5) is still open, but
the fields argue for it now: annual mean temperature at sea level varies by
barely 1 °C across the whole domain while elevation swings it by 18 °C, so a
temperature choropleth is essentially a re-coloured hypsometric map.
Isolines over hillshade carry the same information with less redundancy.

## 8. Open follow-ups

* Precipitation only decays leeward within the domain; the model has no
  moisture budget, so a second range downwind is never starved. Minor over
  130 km, and stated rather than fixed.
* Non-dimensional mountain height `Nm·H/U ≈ 1.4` at the Spine — at the edge
  of where flow blocking starts to matter, so the model likely overstates
  crest precipitation and understates upwind-flank precipitation. A blocking
  correction (Smith 2003 / Barstad & Smith 2005) is the obvious extension if
  Tappa 5 turns out sensitive to it.
* Snow metrics (Tappa 3) should be precipitation-aware, not a pure
  temperature threshold — see §5.
* Re-check `lapse_seasonal_amplitude_c_per_km` in Tappa 3; it moves summit
  seasonality and therefore the permanent-snow area.
* `validation_reference.transect` in `config/parameters.yml` is still `null`.
  The comparisons in §5 are station-based rather than transect-based, which
  turned out to be sufficient; either fill the field with the stations used
  or drop it.
* Tappa 1 §13's coastal micro-warp remains deliberately unimplemented, and
  nothing here changes that. Tappa 1 §13's `base_lift_m` follow-up is now
  moot (§0).
